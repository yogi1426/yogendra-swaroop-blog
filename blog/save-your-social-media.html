Save Your Social Media

How account takeovers actually happen — phishing and brute force — and the two controls that stop most of them.

Jan 3, 2020 4 min read

Thank you for coming here — hopefully you'll learn at least something from this post. It's for everyone, techie or non-techie, and it's about your security on the internet.

A few days back, a friend of mine called and said "my Insta account is hacked." I didn't take it seriously at first — of course, why would anyone hack his account? I checked, and yes, it was compromised. I asked for his last password and found it was easily guessable if you knew him personally.

So here comes one question: how did the attacker compromise his account?

"Arguing that you don't care about the right to privacy because you have nothing to hide is no different than saying you don't care about free speech because you have nothing to say." — Edward Snowden

It's somewhat our responsibility to stay safe online, and it completely depends on us. The first thing I ask anyone who says their account was hacked is: how complex was your password, and did you have two-factor authentication enabled?

How many of us actually take the "make your password complex" warning seriously? I'd guess about half. How many of us use a second layer of security? Close to none.

How accounts actually get taken over

As far as I know, there are two common ways an attacker gets access to your account:

  1. Phishing
  2. Brute force (hit and trial)

Phishing

Phishing is the technique where an attacker sends you a malformed page that looks similar to a social media login page (Facebook, Gmail, Instagram, etc.) and asks you to log in there. Because you trust the look of the page, you log in with your credentials — and boom, your credentials go to the attacker while you're redirected to the real site. The attacker simply leverages your trust in the page's appearance.

Takeaway: never trust a random link asking you to log in — verify the URL first.

Brute force

As the name suggests, this attack works when your password isn't complex — plain words, dictionary terms, or anything easy to guess. The attacker runs a wordlist against your password, and if it matches, your account is taken. This attack is less successful against strong passwords, but it does exist.

Takeaway: use a complex, unique password.

The one control that changes everything

To add a real layer of security, implement two-factor authentication (2FA) — you get an OTP on your phone or email, and only then are you granted access. Even if your password leaks, 2FA stops most takeovers cold.

If your account was compromised anyway, contact the platform's support — they will help. And this really is the only path forward, because once an account is hacked, the attacker won't fall for a phishing bait or anything similar aimed at getting it back.

Thank you for your time. Please do share and stay safe.
